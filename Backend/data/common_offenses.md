# LAWoud Common Offences Knowledge Base

Common criminal offences that come up in everyday disputes — neighbour and
property fights, money lending, matrimonial conflicts, reputation harm. Each
entry gives the Indian Penal Code (IPC) reference alongside the Bharatiya
Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023 equivalent, since BNS applies to offences committed
on or after 1 July 2024 while older cases still run under the IPC.

Sections are split by `##` headings with optional `**Topic:**`,
`**Keywords:**`, `**Law:**`, and `**Source:**` metadata lines, the same
format as `legal_knowledge.md`.

## Cheque Bounce — Section 138, Negotiable Instruments Act

**Topic:** Cheque dishonour / Section 138 NI Act
**Keywords:** cheque bounce, cheque dishonour, section 138, negotiable instruments, insufficient funds, cheque return, payment stopped, demand notice
**Law:** Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, Section 138
**Source:** India Code | https://www.indiacode.nic.in/

Dishonour of a cheque for insufficient funds (or because payment was stopped,
or the account was closed) is a criminal offence under Section 138 of the
Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 — in addition to the civil remedy of suing
to recover the money. It is one of the most common offences in everyday money
disputes between individuals, landlords/tenants, and small businesses.

- The cheque must have been issued to discharge a legally enforceable debt or
  liability — a cheque given as a gift or security may not attract Section 138.
- The payee must present the cheque within its validity period (three months
  from the date on the cheque).
- After the bank returns the cheque unpaid, the payee must send a written
  demand notice to the drawer within 30 days of receiving the cheque return
  memo, demanding payment within 15 days.
- If the drawer does not pay within those 15 days, a criminal complaint can
  be filed before a Magistrate within one month of the cause of action
  arising.
- On conviction, the drawer can be punished with imprisonment up to two
  years, or a fine up to twice the cheque amount, or both.
- Section 138 cases are compoundable — the parties can settle at any stage,
  and courts actively encourage settlement, often at the cost of the drawer
  paying the cheque amount plus expenses.
- Keep the returned cheque, the bank's return memo, and proof that the
  demand notice was sent and delivered — these are the core documents.

## Dowry Demands and Dowry Harassment

**Topic:** Dowry demand / cruelty by husband or in-laws
**Keywords:** dowry, dowry demand, dowry harassment, 498a, cruelty by husband, in-laws harassment, dowry prohibition, stridhan
**Law:** Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961 (Sections 3 and 4); BNS Section 85/86 (previously IPC Section 498A)
**Source:** India Code | https://www.indiacode.nic.in/

Giving, taking, or even demanding dowry is an offence under the Dowry
Prohibition Act, 1961. Separately, cruelty by a husband or his relatives —
which includes harassment linked to dowry demands — is a criminal offence
under Section 85 of the BNS (the former IPC Section 498A), punishable with
imprisonment up to three years and a fine.

- "Cruelty" covers conduct likely to drive a woman to suicide or cause grave
  injury, and harassment to coerce her or her family into meeting unlawful
  dowry demands (BNS Section 86, previously the IPC 498A explanation).
- Demanding dowry directly or indirectly, at or any time after marriage, is
  itself punishable under Section 4 of the Dowry Prohibition Act.
- A woman facing such harassment can file a police complaint/FIR, approach a
  Magistrate directly, or seek relief under the Protection of Women from
  Domestic Violence Act, 2005 (protection orders, residence orders, monetary
  relief) — the civil and criminal routes can run together.
- Stridhan (the woman's own property, including gifts received at marriage)
  remains her property; withholding it can also be complained about.
- Free legal aid is available to women through the Legal Services
  Authorities; NALSA's helpline is 15100, and the National Commission for
  Women helpline is 7827170170.

## Dowry Death

**Topic:** Dowry death
**Keywords:** dowry death, 304b, death of wife, bride burning, dowry suicide, unnatural death within seven years
**Law:** BNS Section 80 (previously IPC Section 304B)
**Source:** India Code | https://www.indiacode.nic.in/

Where a woman dies from burns, bodily injury, or otherwise than in normal
circumstances within seven years of marriage, and it is shown that she was
subjected to cruelty or harassment over dowry soon before her death, the law
treats it as a "dowry death" — the husband or relative responsible can be
punished with imprisonment of at least seven years, which may extend to life
imprisonment.

- The court can presume the accused caused the death once the basic
  conditions are shown — the burden shifts to the accused to explain.
- Such deaths are investigated through an inquest, usually by a Magistrate
  or senior police officer, and the family should ensure the post-mortem
  and dying declaration (if any) are properly recorded.
- Related offences such as dowry harassment and abetment of suicide can be
  charged alongside.

## Theft

**Topic:** Theft of property
**Keywords:** theft, stolen, stolen goods, robbery, house theft, phone stolen, theft complaint, bns 303
**Law:** BNS Section 303 (previously IPC Sections 378 and 379)
**Source:** India Code | https://www.indiacode.nic.in/

Theft is dishonestly taking movable property out of another person's
possession without their consent. It is a cognizable offence — the police
must register an FIR and can investigate without prior court permission.

- Punishment for theft can extend to three years' imprisonment, a fine, or
  both; aggravated forms (theft in a dwelling house, theft by a servant,
  preparation to cause hurt) carry heavier punishment.
- Report the theft to the police station with jurisdiction as soon as
  possible, with a list of stolen items, approximate value, and any
  identifying details (serial numbers, IMEI for phones, photographs).
- Snatching of property is a separate, more serious offence under BNS
  Section 304, and robbery (theft combined with violence or fear of it) is
  dealt with under BNS Section 309.
- If police refuse to register an FIR, a complaint can be escalated to the
  Superintendent of Police or filed before a Magistrate.

## Assault, Criminal Force and Causing Hurt

**Topic:** Physical assault in everyday disputes
**Keywords:** assault, beaten up, physical fight, criminal force, hurt, grievous hurt, slapped, attacked, neighbour fight, bns 130, bns 131
**Law:** BNS Sections 128–131 (previously IPC Sections 349–352); BNS Sections 114–117 for hurt (previously IPC 319–325)
**Source:** India Code | https://www.indiacode.nic.in/

"Assault" is a gesture or preparation that makes another person apprehend
immediate criminal force; actually using force without consent is "criminal
force". Causing bodily pain, disease, or infirmity is "hurt" — and it becomes
"grievous hurt" in defined serious cases such as fractures, disfigurement, or
loss of sight or hearing.

- Simple assault or criminal force (for example a shove or slap in a
  quarrel) is punishable with imprisonment up to three months, a fine, or
  both — typically pursued as a private complaint or after police
  intervention.
- Voluntarily causing hurt can attract imprisonment up to one year or a
  fine; voluntarily causing grievous hurt can go up to seven years, and
  higher where weapons or dangerous means are used.
- Get a medical examination done promptly — a medico-legal certificate
  recording injuries is key evidence, even for seemingly minor injuries.
- Preserve any CCTV footage, photos of injuries, and witness details.
- For minor scuffles the matter is often compoundable, but assaults on
  women, public servants on duty, or causing grievous hurt are treated far
  more seriously.

## Criminal Intimidation

**Topic:** Threats and intimidation
**Keywords:** criminal intimidation, threat, threatened, threatening messages, intimidation, blackmail threat, bns 351
**Law:** BNS Section 351 (previously IPC Sections 503 and 506)
**Source:** India Code | https://www.indiacode.nic.in/

Criminal intimidation is threatening another person with injury to their
person, reputation, or property (or to someone they care about) in order to
alarm them or force them to do or not do something.

- A simple threat is punishable with imprisonment up to two years; threats
  of death or grievous hurt, of destruction of property by fire, or made
  anonymously can attract up to seven years.
- Threats sent over WhatsApp, SMS, email, or social media count — preserve
  screenshots, call recordings, and message logs as evidence, and avoid
  deleting the thread.
- Intimidation to stop someone from filing a complaint, vacate a property,
  or withdraw a case is itself an offence independent of the underlying
  dispute.

## Defamation

**Topic:** Defamation — harm to reputation
**Keywords:** defamation, reputation, false accusation, slander, libel, defamatory post, character assassination, bns 356
**Law:** BNS Section 356 (previously IPC Sections 499 and 500)
**Source:** India Code | https://www.indiacode.nic.in/

Defamation is making or publishing an imputation about a person — by words,
signs, or visible representations — intending or knowing it will harm their
reputation. In India it is both a criminal offence and a civil wrong for
which damages can be claimed.

- Criminal defamation can be punished with imprisonment up to two years, a
  fine, both, or community service.
- There are important exceptions — statements that are true and made for
  the public good, good-faith opinions on public conduct, fair reporting of
  court proceedings, and genuine cautioning are generally not defamation.
- Defamation includes posts on social media, WhatsApp groups, and other
  online publications — preserve screenshots with dates and the identity of
  the account that posted.
- A criminal complaint is filed before a Magistrate; a civil suit for
  damages is a separate remedy that can also be pursued.

## Cheating

**Topic:** Cheating and fraudulent inducement
**Keywords:** cheating, cheated, fraud, deceived, false promise, advance money not returned, bns 318, cheating complaint
**Law:** BNS Section 318 (previously IPC Sections 415–420)
**Source:** India Code | https://www.indiacode.nic.in/

Cheating is deceiving someone into handing over property or money, or into
doing or not doing something they otherwise would not have — for example
taking an advance for goods or services never intended to be delivered, or
inducing payment on a knowingly false promise.

- Cheating in general is punishable with imprisonment up to three years;
  cheating that induces delivery of property (the common "took my money and
  disappeared" case) can attract up to seven years and a fine.
- The dishonest intention must generally exist from the start — a
  transaction that merely went wrong later is usually a civil dispute, not
  cheating. The distinction between a civil breach and a criminal cheat is
  the single most contested issue in these complaints.
- Preserve the payment proofs, agreements, chats, and advertisements that
  show what was promised.
- If police treat the complaint as purely civil, a private complaint can
  still be filed before a Magistrate, and a civil recovery suit remains
  available in parallel.
